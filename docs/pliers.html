How we rank

Every tool gets one score from 0 to 100, weighted 45% on its star rating, 35% on how many people have reviewed it (more reviews = more confidence the rating is real), and 20% on the features that matter for the job. The list is ordered by that score. Best Overall is our pick for the best all-around, ready-to-use tool; Best Budget the best value at or under $17; and Money No Object the one to buy if price is no object.

The features score is built from dual-material handles (30%), groove-joint pliers (25%), cutting pliers in the set (25%), a storage pouch (20%), capped at 100. The review-count score is on a log scale, so each tenfold jump in reviews adds the same amount, and 15,000 or more reviews gets full marks. Open Why this score on any tool to see its numbers.

Buyer's guide

What pliers does everyone actually need?

Four cover most jobs: slip-joint (general gripping), groove-joint or water-pump (adjustable, for pipe and big nuts), long-nose (reaching into tight spots and bending wire), and diagonal cutters (for wire). A linesman plier is the fifth if you do electrical work. A good mixed set gives you all of these at once; buy individual pliers only to fill a gap.

What are groove-joint (water-pump) pliers for?

Groove-joint pliers — also called tongue-and-groove, water-pump, or by the brand name Channellock — have a slotted pivot that lets the jaws open to several widths while staying parallel. That makes them the tool for gripping round things: pipe, large nuts, fittings. A 10-inch pair is the single most useful plier for plumbing and general home repair.

Are premium pliers like Knipex worth it?

For heavy or daily use, yes. Knipex Cobras self-lock onto the work so they don't slip and round off a nut, adjust with one hand, and last a lifetime. For occasional home projects, a $20 mixed set does the job fine. Spend the money if pliers are a tool you use hard and often; otherwise a value set is smart.

Do the cutting edges matter?

They do if you cut wire. Look for induction-hardened cutting edges — they stay sharp far longer than soft steel and give clean cuts instead of crushing the wire. Cheap pliers with soft jaws dull fast and can nick or mangle wire, which matters for electrical work. Most reputable sets now harden the edges; it's worth confirming.

Slip-joint or groove-joint for general gripping?

A slip-joint plier has two jaw positions and is compact and light — great for small everyday gripping and loosening. A groove-joint plier is bigger, opens much wider, and grips round objects far better, but it's bulkier. Most people want both: a slip-joint for quick small jobs and a groove-joint for anything large or round.
