Buyer's guide

How hot does a heat gun need to get?

Around 1,000°F handles paint stripping, adhesive removal, and loosening rusted bolts. Lower heat, roughly 200 to 600°F, is for shrink tubing, vinyl wrap, bending PVC, and plastics you don't want to scorch. A dial or digital control covers both with one tool; a two-setting gun gives you one low and one high.

Dual temperature or variable temperature?

Dual-temperature guns (Genesis, BLACK+DECKER, Wagner HT1000) have a low and a high switch. They're cheap and fine for paint and pipes. Variable guns (SEEKONE, PORTER-CABLE, CRAFTSMAN) add a dial, and digital guns (Wagner FURNO 500, DEWALT, Makita) let you set an exact temperature and repeat it, which matters for vinyl wrap, tinting, and plastics.

Do I need nozzles?

They help. A glass protector deflects heat when you strip window frames, a flat nozzle spreads heat along a strip of paint, a reflector wraps heat around a pipe or shrink tube, and a reducer concentrates it on a small spot. Several guns here ship bare; the Genesis, SEEKONE, AcmyslaT, Wagner FURNO 500, and the DEWALT and Makita kits include nozzles.

What about the small 300 to 400 watt craft heat guns?

Those are embossing and resin tools. They're light and cheap, but at a quarter of the wattage they won't strip paint or thaw a pipe in any reasonable time. We left them off this list; buy one only for paper crafts or epoxy.

Is a heat gun safe to use indoors?

Yes, with care. The nozzle stays hot for minutes after you switch off, so use the built-in stand and let it cool on low or in cool-down mode. When stripping old paint, keep the temperature as low as works and test pre-1978 paint for lead first: overheating lead paint releases toxic fumes.

Corded or cordless?

Corded, for almost everyone. A heat gun turns electricity straight into heat, so it draws far more power than a battery pack can supply for long. Cordless guns suit quick shrink-tube jobs away from an outlet; for anything else a corded gun is cheaper and several times stronger.
