How we rank

Every tool gets one score from 0 to 100, weighted 50% on its star rating, 35% on how many people have reviewed it (more reviews = more confidence the rating is real), and 15% on the features that matter for the job. The list is ordered by that score. Best Overall is our pick for the best all-around, ready-to-use tool; Best Budget the best value at or under $50; and Money No Object the one to buy if price is no object.

The features score is built from a variable-speed reversing trigger (30%), a complete kit (25%), a keyless chuck (25%), a hammer-drill mode (20%), capped at 100. The review-count score is on a log scale, so each tenfold jump in reviews adds the same amount, and 60,000 or more reviews gets full marks. Open Why this score on any tool to see its numbers.

Buyer's guide

Why corded over cordless?

A corded drill never runs out of charge and gives full torque for the least money — great for long sessions, mixing paint or mortar, and a keep-forever bench drill. The trade is the cord and needing an outlet. Want to drill anywhere? See the cordless list.

Drill/driver or hammer drill?

A regular drill/driver bores wood, metal, and plastic and drives screws. A hammer drill adds a rapid pounding action for drilling into concrete, brick, and masonry. If you'll never touch masonry, save money and skip the hammer feature.

3/8 inch or 1/2 inch chuck?

A 3/8" chuck fits most common bits and spins faster — best for general drilling and driving. A 1/2" chuck accepts larger bits (spade bits, hole saws, big masonry bits) and usually has more low-end torque. Bigger jobs, get 1/2".

Keyed or keyless chuck?

A keyless chuck lets you swap bits by hand — fast and convenient. A keyed chuck grips slightly harder for heavy drilling but needs the chuck key (which you'll eventually lose). For most people, keyless wins.

How many amps?

For a corded drill, amps roughly track power. 5-6 amps is fine for light home drilling; 7-8 amps handles general work and bigger bits; 10 amps is heavy-duty hammer-drill territory. Ignore inflated wattage claims on no-name tools.
