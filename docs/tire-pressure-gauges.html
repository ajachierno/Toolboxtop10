How we rank

Every tool gets one score from 0 to 100, weighted 45% on its star rating, 35% on how many people have reviewed it (more reviews = more confidence the rating is real), and 20% on the features that matter for the job. The list is ordered by that score. Best Overall is our pick for the best all-around, ready-to-use tool; Best Budget the best value at or under $7; and Money No Object the one to buy if price is no object.

The features score is built from accuracy 1 (30%), ansi (20%), bleeder (20%), lit display (15%), battery free (15%), capped at 100. The review-count score is on a log scale, so each tenfold jump in reviews adds the same amount, and 30,000 or more reviews gets full marks. Open Why this score on any tool to see its numbers.

Buyer's guide

Digital, dial or pencil?

Digital gauges are easiest to read and usually accurate to about 1 PSI. Dial gauges need no batteries and many glow in the dark. Pencil gauges are cheap glove-box backups but read to a few PSI.

What accuracy should I look for?

Look for a stated figure: ±1 PSI or ±1% is good. ANSI B40.1 Grade A or B40.7 Grade 2A means the gauge was calibrated to a recognized standard.

What range?

Cars run 30 to 40 PSI and spares about 60. RVs, trailers and heavy trucks can need 80 to 120 PSI or more, so pick a gauge that reads above your highest tire.

Why a bleed valve?

A bleeder lets you let air out a little at a time while the gauge stays on the valve, so you can hit the exact pressure.

When to check

Check cold tires, before driving or after the car has sat for a few hours. Use the pressure on the door jamb sticker, not the number on the tire sidewall.
