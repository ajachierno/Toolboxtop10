Buyer's guide

Cordless rotary hammer or cordless hammer drill?

A hammer drill rattles the bit with clutch plates, which is fine for a few small holes in brick. A rotary hammer uses a piston to strike the bit, so it drills concrete several times faster and can chip tile or mortar in hammer-only mode. If you're setting more than a handful of concrete anchors, get the rotary hammer.

Should I buy one that matches my drill's batteries?

Usually, yes. A bare DEWALT, Milwaukee, Makita, or Bosch hammer is the cheapest way in if you already own that brand's packs. If you don't own any, a kit like the Seesii or Dong Cheng includes batteries and a charger and costs less than most bare name-brand tools.

How much impact energy do I need?

About 1.2 to 1.5 J is enough for 1/4" to 3/8" anchor holes, often overhead. 2 to 2.6 J handles 1/2" to 3/4" holes and light chipping. Brands don't all measure it the same way, and some list foot-pounds instead of joules (1 ft-lb is about 1.36 J), so compare within a brand when you can.

What's the difference between 5/8", 3/4", and 1" tools?

That's the largest concrete hole each tool is rated for. The small 5/8" and 11/16" hammers are lighter and built for overhead anchor work. The 1" tools drill bigger holes faster and usually include a chisel mode, but they weigh more.

Is a clutch or kickback control worth having?

Yes. When a bit hits rebar or binds, a rotary hammer can twist hard enough to hurt your wrist. A clutch slips, and electronic systems like Milwaukee's AUTOSTOP or Bosch's kickback control cut power. On this list, the Milwaukee 2912-20, Bosch, Seesii, and ACROPOW listings state one.

Do I need special bits?

Yes. Every tool here takes SDS-Plus bits and chisels, which lock in without a key. Regular round-shank bits need an adapter chuck, and SDS-Max bits are a larger size that won't fit.
