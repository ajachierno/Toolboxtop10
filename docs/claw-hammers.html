Buyer's guide

What weight claw hammer should I buy?

A 16 oz hammer is the right choice for most people — light enough for all-day control on general repairs, trim, and light nailing. Step up to 20–22 oz only if you drive a lot of big framing nails, where the extra head weight sinks a nail in fewer swings. Heavier isn't better for everyday work; it just tires your arm and can overdrive small nails. An 8 oz is for pictures and tacks, not construction.

Curved claw or straight (rip) claw?

A curved claw gives more leverage for pulling nails, which makes it the better all-purpose choice for repairs and remodeling. A straight (rip) claw is flatter and works like a small pry bar — better for demolition, splitting boards, and getting into tight spots, but it pulls nails with less leverage. If you mostly hang and repair, buy curved; if you tear things apart, buy rip.

Smooth face or milled (waffle) face?

A smooth face is the default and the only choice for finish work — it won't leave marks if you graze the wood. A milled face has a waffle pattern that bites the nail head so the hammer won't skate off when you're driving framing nails fast. The trade-off is it stamps that waffle pattern into anything it touches, so keep milled faces on the framing site and smooth faces near trim.

Steel, fiberglass, or wood handle?

One-piece forged steel (like Estwing) is the most durable — the head can never fly off — but it transmits the most shock, which good grips offset. Fiberglass is the value sweet spot: it dampens vibration, resists weather, and won't rot. Wood feels great and absorbs shock naturally but can loosen or crack over time. For a buy-it-once tool pick steel; for the best value pick fiberglass.

Is a magnetic nail starter worth it?

Yes, if you often nail one-handed or overhead. A magnetic slot holds the nail so you can set it with a first tap without pinching it between your fingers — safer and faster on ladders and in awkward spots. It's a real convenience, not a gimmick, though a plain hammer does the same job once the nail is started.

Why does one-piece construction matter?

On a two-piece hammer, the head is fixed to a separate handle, and years of hard strikes can eventually work that joint loose — a loose head is dangerous. A one-piece forged hammer is a single bar of steel from face to grip, so there's no joint to fail. It's the reason pros who swing a hammer daily tend to buy one-piece steel.
