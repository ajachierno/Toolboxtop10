How we rank

Every tool gets one score from 0 to 100, weighted 45% on its star rating, 35% on how many people have reviewed it (more reviews = more confidence the rating is real), and 20% on the features that matter for the job. The list is ordered by that score. Best Overall is our pick for the best all-around, ready-to-use tool; Best Budget the best value at or under $45; and Money No Object the one to buy if price is no object.

The features score is built from depth adjust (20%), jam release (20%), oil free (20%), selectable trigger (20%), a carrying case (20%), capped at 100. The review-count score is on a log scale, so each tenfold jump in reviews adds the same amount, and 5,000 or more reviews gets full marks. Open Why this score on any tool to see its numbers.

Buyer's guide

Brad nailer or finish nailer?

An 18-gauge brad nailer shoots thin nails that leave tiny holes, ideal for trim, casing and small molding. A 16-gauge finish nailer shoots thicker, longer nails for baseboard, door jambs and heavier trim. Most people start with a brad nailer.

What size compressor do I need?

Brad nailers use very little air, well under 1 CFM. Any pancake or small hot-dog compressor runs one easily. See our air compressor list.

Oil-free or oiled?

Oil-free nailers don't need a few drops of oil each day and won't spit oil onto finished wood. Oiled nailers are fine for paint-grade trim; keep a rag handy.

Sequential or bump fire?

Sequential (single) mode fires one nail per trigger pull with the nose pressed: safer and more precise. Bump (contact) mode fires each time the nose touches with the trigger held, which is faster for repetitive work. A selectable trigger gives you both.

Is a 2-in-1 nailer/stapler worth it?

If you'll staple upholstery, drawer bottoms or screen as well as nail trim, yes. Brads and 1/4-inch narrow crown staples share a magazine width, so the combo tools work well. Avoid tools that also claim 16-gauge nails.
