Buyer's guide

What is a brad nailer for?

An 18-gauge brad nailer drives thin nails for trim, baseboard, casing, cabinet face frames, and small moldings. The small head leaves a hole you can fill with a dab of putty. It doesn't hold well enough for framing or anything structural.

Brad nailer or finish nailer?

Brad nailers use 18-gauge nails, usually up to 2 in long. Finish nailers use thicker 15- or 16-gauge nails up to 2-1/2 in and hold heavy trim and door casing better, but leave a bigger hole. For most baseboard and window trim, an 18-gauge brad nailer is enough.

Do cordless nailers have a delay before they fire?

Older flywheel designs had a short ramp-up while the motor spun up. Air-spring and mechanical-spring tools like the Milwaukee 2746 and the Metabo HPT nailers advertise no ramp-up time, which matters most when you're nailing fast along a long run of trim.

What's the difference between sequential and contact firing?

Sequential fires one nail each time you press the nose down and then pull the trigger, which is slower but more precise. Contact (bump) mode fires every time the nose touches the work while you hold the trigger. Several listings above don't mention a mode switch at all.

Kit or bare tool?

If you already have DEWALT 20V, Milwaukee M18, or Ryobi ONE+ batteries, the bare tool saves money. If you don't, a kit is cheaper than buying a tool, battery, and charger separately. Each brand's battery only fits that brand's tools.

Are the cheap 4V staple-gun combos real brad nailers?

They fire short brads, usually 9/16 to 5/8 in, which is fine for picture frames and crafts. They can't drive the 1-1/4 to 2 in brads that baseboard and door casing need.
