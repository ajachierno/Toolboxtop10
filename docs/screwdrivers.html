How we rank

The list is ordered by that score. The features score is built from magnetic tips (30%), a ratcheting handle (25%), nut drivers (25%), a carrying case (20%), capped at 100. The review-count score is on a log scale, so each tenfold jump in reviews adds the same amount, and 20,000 or more reviews gets full marks. Open Why this score on any tool to see its numbers.

Buyer's guide

Individual drivers or a multi-bit set?

Individual drivers give the best grip and the most torque, and there's no bit to slip out under load — the choice if you drive screws hard or often. A multi-bit driver (like the Klein 11-in-1) replaces a whole set with one tool that fits in a pocket, at the cost of swapping bits. Most people are well served by a good multi-bit for everyday jobs and a small fixed set for heavy work.

Is a ratcheting screwdriver worth it?

For repetitive driving, yes. A ratchet lets you keep the bit seated and just rock the handle back and forth instead of re-gripping every turn, which is much faster on a run of screws. The trade-off is a little extra length and a mechanism that can wear over years. If you assemble furniture or drive a lot of fasteners by hand, it's a real upgrade.

Do magnetic tips matter?

They help a lot. A magnetic tip holds the screw on the driver so you can start it one-handed and in awkward spots without dropping it. It's especially useful overhead or in tight cavities. Most cheap sets are magnetic; some premium fixed drivers aren't, though you can magnetize any tip with a magnetizer tool.

What tip types do I actually need?

Phillips and slotted (flat) cover the majority of household screws. Add Torx (star-shaped) for electronics, appliances, and a lot of modern furniture, and square (Robertson) if you work with decking or cabinetry. A set that includes all four, or a multi-bit that does, future-proofs you against the odd fastener.

Do I need a precision set too?

If you ever open a phone, laptop, game console, glasses, or a watch, yes — those use tiny Torx, tri-wing, and pentalobe screws that a normal driver can't touch. A precision kit is cheap and does what a full-size set can't. It won't replace a regular set for household screws, so most people end up owning both.

  • The '415 pieces' is marketing math — it's mostly sockets, tape, cable ties, and assorted hardware. The actual screwdrivers are a small, generic subset, so you're paying $79 for a box, not for good drivers.
