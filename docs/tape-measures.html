The features score is built from an auto-locking blade (30%), a magnetic hook (25%), fraction markings (25%), double-sided markings (20%), capped at 100. Buyer's guide

How long a tape measure do I need?

A 25-foot tape is the standard for general building and DIY — it reaches across most rooms and handles lumber and framing. A 16-foot tape is lighter and fine for furniture, crafts, and around-the-house jobs. Go longer than 25 feet only for specific work like surveying a large space; a 30- or 35-foot tape is heavier and bulkier to carry all day.

What is 'standout' and why does it matter?

Standout is how far you can extend the blade horizontally before it folds over under its own weight. A stiff 11–13 foot standout lets you measure across a room or reach up a wall alone; a floppy 6–7 foot standout means you need a helper or a wall to catch the end. It's the single biggest difference between a cheap tape and a pro one.

Auto-lock or manual (thumb) lock?

A manual lock is a slide you push to hold the blade where you want it. An auto-lock (self-lock) holds the blade out automatically and only retracts when you press a button — handy when your hands are full, though it means an extra press to reel it in. Both are fine; auto-lock saves your thumb on repetitive measuring.

Is a magnetic hook worth it?

Yes, if you measure metal alone. A magnetic tip grabs onto steel studs, ductwork, and pipe so you can pull a measurement without a second person or a nail to catch on. It does nothing on wood or drywall, so a carpenter may not need it — but for HVAC, metal framing, and mechanical work it's a real time-saver.

Do I want fractions printed on the blade?

Easy-read tapes print the fractional marks (1/8, 1/4, 1/2) as labeled numbers instead of just tick lines, often in alternating colors. If you don't read a tape every day, it removes the guesswork and the mis-reads. Pros who know a tape by feel often don't need it, but for most people it's a genuine convenience worth having.

Does a nylon-coated blade actually last longer?

It does. The first few inches of a tape take the most abuse — dragged across concrete, scraped on metal — and that's where the printed markings wear off first. A nylon or Mylar coating protects the print and resists rust, so a coated blade stays readable far longer than a bare steel one. It's worth a couple of extra dollars on a tape you'll use hard.
