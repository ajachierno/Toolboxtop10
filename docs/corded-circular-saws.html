How we rank

Every tool gets one score from 0 to 100, weighted 50% on its star rating, 35% on how many people have reviewed it (more reviews = more confidence the rating is real), and 15% on the features that matter for the job. The list is ordered by that score. Best Overall is our pick for the best all-around, ready-to-use tool; Best Budget the best value at or under $60; and Money No Object the one to buy if price is no object.

The features score is built from a complete kit (35%), an electric blade brake (25%), a laser guide (20%), a high bevel capacity (20%), capped at 100. The review-count score is on a log scale, so each tenfold jump in reviews adds the same amount, and 60,000 or more reviews gets full marks. Open Why this score on any tool to see its numbers.

Buyer's guide

Why corded over cordless?

A corded saw gives you full, sustained power for the lowest price and never needs a battery — ideal for a fixed shop, long rip cuts, or if you don't want to buy into a battery platform. The trade is the cord: you need an outlet and you're managing a cable.

How many amps do I need?

15 amps is full power and what the best saws use. 13-14 amps is a small step down that's fine for occasional home use. Under about 11 amps you're into budget or compact territory that struggles with thick or wet lumber.

What blade size?

7-1/4 inch is the full-size standard and cuts through 2x lumber in a single pass. 4-1/2 inch compact saws are for plywood, trim, and flooring — handy and light, but they can't crosscut framing lumber.

Sidewinder vs worm drive?

A sidewinder (in-line) saw is lighter and cheaper — what most people should buy. A worm-drive saw gears the motor for more torque and a front-heavy balance pros like for rip cuts and wet lumber; heavier and pricier.

Does an electric brake matter?

An electric brake stops the spinning blade in a second or two when you release the trigger. It's a real safety feature — one reason the DEWALT DWE575SB is our top corded pick.

This isn't a circular saw — it's a 4-inch angle grinder with a saw guard bolted on. It cuts 5/8 of an inch deep and spins at grinder speed. Wrong, and genuinely unsafe, for the work a circular saw does.
