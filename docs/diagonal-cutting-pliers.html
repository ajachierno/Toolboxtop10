How we rank

Every tool gets one score from 0 to 100, weighted 45% on its star rating, 35% on how many people have reviewed it (more reviews = more confidence the rating is real), and 20% on the features that matter for the job. The list is ordered by that score. Best Overall is the top-scoring pick with at least 4.0 stars and 30 reviews (a ready-to-use kit wins a close call); Best Budget is the top score at or under $13 that costs less than Best Overall; and Money No Object is the one to buy if price is no object.

The features score is built from high leverage (35%), hardened edges (30%), comfort grips (20%), flush cut (15%), capped at 100. The review-count score is on a log scale, so each tenfold jump in reviews adds the same amount, and 200 or more reviews gets full marks. A listing under two years old with at least 30 reviews has its count scaled up for the shorter time it has had to collect them, never more than double. Buyer's guide

What's the difference between diagonal cutters and flush cutters?

Diagonal cutters have beveled blades that leave a slight point on the cut end and handle copper, steel and tie wire. Flush cutters have one flat side that leaves a flat end, which is what you want on zip ties and electronics leads, but they're built for small, soft wire. The Klein D275-5 on this page is a flush cutter rated to 16 AWG.

Do I need high-leverage cutters?

If you often cut 12 AWG copper, screws or hard wire, yes. A high-leverage design moves the rivet closer to the blades so the same squeeze cuts harder. Klein claims 36% more cutting power for the D228-8, and Crescent claims 50% for its compound-action PS5429C. For occasional cuts, a standard 6- or 7-inch pair is lighter and fits tighter spots.

What size should I buy?

Six to seven inches suits most household electrical and DIY work. Eight-inch pairs give more leverage for heavier wire, and the 9-inch Klein D248-9ST is meant for rebar tie wire. Five-inch flush cutters are for zip ties and electronics.

What does HRC mean on the cutting edge?

It's the Rockwell C hardness of the blade. Higher numbers hold an edge longer and cut harder wire. The KNIPEX 74 22 200 lists about 64 HRC, the KNIPEX 70 01 160 about 62 HRC, and WORKPRO lists up to 65 HRC for its high-frequency-hardened edge. Several brands here say only "induction hardened" without a number.

Can I cut live wires with these?

No. None of the pliers on this page are listed as 1000V insulated, so shut off the circuit first. Insulated diagonal cutters carry a 1000V rating on the handles and are sold separately.
