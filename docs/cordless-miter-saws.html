Buyer's guide

Is a cordless miter saw powerful enough?

For trim, flooring, and most 2x framing, yes. DEWALT rates the 7-1/4" DCS361 at about 183 cuts of 2x4 pine per charge, and the 12" DCS785 cuts 14 inches wide. Where a cordless saw falls short is all-day production cutting on one or two batteries. If the saw lives on a bench next to an outlet, a corded saw costs less for the same capacity.

Should I buy the kit or the bare tool?

Buy the bare tool if you already own batteries on that platform. The saw is the expensive part, and a pack you already have works fine. If you're starting from zero, a kit like the DEWALT DCS361M1 adds a 4.0Ah battery and charger for about $83 over the bare tool.

What blade size do I need?

7-1/4" covers trim and 2x lumber, and the sliding versions crosscut a 2x10 or 2x12. A 10" or 12" saw cuts taller base molding and wider boards but weighs 34 to 52 pounds. A 6-1/2" saw like the Makita XSL05Z is a trim tool, sized for baseboard and casing.

Sliding or fixed?

A slider pulls the blade through the board, so a small blade crosscuts wide stock. The 7-1/4" RYOBI PBT01B crosscuts a 2x10, while the fixed 10" DEWALT DCS714 stops at 6 inches wide. Fixed saws are lighter and more compact, which is why trim carpenters like them.

Does the battery platform matter?

More than any spec. A saw that runs on packs you already own saves the cost of batteries and a charger. Avoid saws with a proprietary battery that fits nothing else, like the VEVOR on this page: when that battery dies, you're shopping for a part that may not be sold separately.

Single or dual bevel?

A single-bevel saw tilts one way, so for the opposite bevel you flip the board. That's fine for framing. Dual bevel saves time on crown and long runs of matched trim. Here, the DEWALT DCS785 and DCS714 and the Makita XSL05Z and XSL06PT bevel both ways.
