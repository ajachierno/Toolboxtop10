How we rank

Every tool gets one score from 0 to 100, weighted 45% on its star rating, 35% on how many people have reviewed it (more reviews = more confidence the rating is real), and 20% on the features that matter for the job. The list is ordered by that score. Best Overall is our pick for the best all-around, ready-to-use tool; Best Budget the best value at or under $60; and Money No Object the one to buy if price is no object.

The features score is built from unthinned (30%), turbine base (25%), flow control (25%), brass nozzles (20%), capped at 100. The review-count score is on a log scale, so each tenfold jump in reviews adds the same amount, and 10,000 or more reviews gets full marks. Open Why this score on any tool to see its numbers.

Buyer's guide

What does HVLP mean?

High volume, low pressure: a turbine blows lots of air at low pressure to atomize paint. You get less overspray and bounce-back than with airless sprayers, which makes HVLP good for furniture, cabinets and trim. It's slower than airless on whole houses.

Do I have to thin the paint?

With most handheld HVLPs, yes, especially for latex. Use the viscosity cup to check. Wagner's FLEXiO models and Graco's EVO Plus are sold as spraying unthinned paint with the right nozzle.

Handheld or separate turbine?

Handhelds put the motor and paint in your hand, which gets heavy. Turbine systems like the Fuji Semi-PRO and Wagner Control Spray Max keep the motor on the floor with a 15 to 25 foot hose, so the gun stays light and the air stays cooler and drier.

What's the cleanup like?

Expect 10 to 20 minutes: run water (or the right solvent) through the gun, then take the nozzle apart and brush it. Paint left to dry in the nozzle is the most common reason sprayers stop working.

How do I avoid runs and orange peel?

Practice on cardboard, keep the gun 6 to 10 inches away and moving, overlap each pass by half, and spray thin coats. Orange peel usually means paint too thick or too little air.
