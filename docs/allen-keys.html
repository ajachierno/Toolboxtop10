Buyer's guide

L-keys, folding, or T-handles?

L-keys are the cheapest and most compact, and a big set covers every size — the default for most people. Folding sets keep the keys captive in a jackknife body so nothing gets lost and you get some leverage. T-handles give the most torque and speed for repetitive work, but take up the most room and usually cover fewer sizes. Many people own an L-key set plus a folder for the bag.

Do I need SAE and metric?

Yes, unless you know your gear is one or the other. Bikes, European furniture, and most electronics are metric; a lot of older American equipment is SAE (inch). A close SAE and metric size can look identical but will round off the fastener if it's the wrong one, so a set with both units clearly labeled saves you grief.

What's a ball end for?

A ball end lets you insert the key at an angle — up to about 25 degrees off-axis — and still turn the fastener, which is a lifesaver when something blocks a straight approach. The trade-off is that the ball contacts less of the socket, so it gives less torque and can slip on a very tight bolt. Break the bolt loose with the flat end, then spin it out with the ball end.

Does the steel quality matter?

It does on tight fasteners. Cheap keys made of soft steel round off at the corners and then round off the screw socket. Look for chrome vanadium or heat-treated alloy steel — it holds its edges and resists twisting. A rounded key or a stripped socket costs far more time than the couple of dollars you saved.

Are hex drill bits the same thing?

No. Hex or 'impact' bits are short 1/4-inch-shank bits made to go in a power drill or impact driver — they give you no leverage in your hand. If you want to turn a hex bolt by hand, you need actual L-keys, a folding set, or T-handles. Buy the drill bits only if you're driving with a power tool.
