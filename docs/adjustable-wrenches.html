How we rank

The list is ordered by that score. Best Overall is the top-scoring pick with at least 4.0 stars and 30 reviews (a ready-to-use kit wins a close call); Best Budget is the top score at or under $13 that costs less than Best Overall; and Money No Object is the one to buy if price is no object.

The features score is built from a wide jaw opening (30%), dual inch/metric scales (25%), a multi-piece set (25%), soft grips (20%), capped at 100. The review-count score is on a log scale, so each tenfold jump in reviews adds the same amount, and 200 or more reviews gets full marks. A listing under two years old with at least 30 reviews has its count scaled up for the shorter time it has had to collect them, never more than double. Buyer's guide

What size adjustable wrench should I buy?

An 8-inch is the single most useful size — big enough for most household and automotive nuts, small enough to control. A 10-inch adds reach and grips larger plumbing fittings. A 6-inch is handy for tight spots and small fasteners, and a 4-inch is really just for tiny work. If you buy one, get an 8-inch; if you buy a set, 6/8/10 covers nearly everything.

Is 'crescent wrench' a brand?

Yes and no. Crescent is an actual tool brand that made adjustable wrenches so popular that the name became the generic term, the way people say 'Kleenex' for tissue. So a 'crescent wrench' just means an adjustable wrench — you don't have to buy the Crescent brand to get one, though they still make good ones.

What's a wide jaw or extra-capacity jaw?

A standard adjustable wrench of a given length only opens so far. Wide-jaw or extra-capacity models open wider for the same handle length, so a compact 8-inch can grip a nut that would normally need a 10-inch. That's a real advantage in tight spaces where a longer handle won't swing. It's worth looking for if you work on plumbing or larger fasteners.

Why do adjustable wrenches round off nuts?

Two reasons: jaw 'creep,' where the movable jaw loosens as you turn and lets the wrench slip, and putting force on the fixed jaw instead of the movable one. Snug the jaw tight before you pull, position the wrench so the pull is toward the movable jaw, and it grips far better. Locking-jaw models eliminate creep entirely. For rounded-off nuts a proper socket or box wrench is always safer.

Do I need the SAE and metric scales on the jaw?

They're a nice convenience, not essential. Laser-etched scales let you dial the wrench to a known bolt size without a separate gauge, which speeds up repetitive work. For occasional use you'll just open the jaw to fit. If you frequently need a specific size, the scales save time; otherwise don't pay much extra for them.
